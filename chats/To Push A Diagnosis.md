# To Push A Diagnosis

## User

My mate needs to be diagnosed, im willing to pay for it. I think ADHD might be a factor. Problem is he refuses to think there is something wrong with him

## Assistant

Here's the wall you're hitting: a diagnosis requires his cooperation. The assessment is hours of interviews and questionnaires about *his* inner experience — you can pay for it, but you can't sit it for him. So the money isn't the problem, the framing is.

And honestly, his framing might be doing more work than you think. "There's something wrong with you" is exactly the pitch that makes people dig in — it asks him to accept a defect before he gets anything in return. Nobody signs up for that. People pursue diagnosis when it promises to *explain* something that's already bothering *them*, not to confirm something bothering their mate.

What actually moves people on this:

**Anchor to his frustrations, not your observations.** If he complains about losing jobs, missing deadlines, forgetting plans, blowing up relationships — that's the door. "You've mentioned X three times this month, there might be a fixable reason for that" lands very differently than "I think you have ADHD." He has to feel the cost himself; you're just connecting his own complaints to a possible cause.

**Kill the "wrong with you" language entirely, including in your own head.** ADHD framing that works: your brain runs on a different fuel system, and knowing that unlocks tools that actually work instead of white-knuckling everything. Framing that doesn't: disorder, problem, fixing him.

**Lower the stakes.** A diagnosis isn't a commitment to medication or a label — it's information he can do nothing with if he chooses. "Worst case you're out an afternoon and you get to tell me I'm wrong" is a much easier yes.

**Then drop it.** Say your piece once, well, and leave it. Pushing converts "I don't think I have it" into "I refuse because you keep pushing" — now it's about the friendship, not the ADHD, and that position is much harder to walk back from.

One uncomfortable possibility to hold: he might be right, or he might not be ready, and both of those are his to be. What you control is making the door easy to walk through — offer standing, no lecture attached — so when a crisis or a bad month makes him curious, the path is already paved and pride doesn't block it.

No clinic on earth assesses a bloke who's dragged in insisting he's fine — the entire assessment is him honestly answering questions about his own head. He stonewalls, the result is garbage. Your money buys nothing without his buy-in. That's not me being soft, that's just how the machine works.

So the real question isn't "how do I get him diagnosed," it's "how do I get him to want it" — and the blunt answer there is: you probably can't, directly. You telling him there's something wrong with him has already failed. Repeating it louder fails harder. That's not cowardice, it's just what happens — people don't get argued into self-examination, they get cornered into it by their own life falling apart.

So your actual options:

1. **Wait for the crash.** Lost job, dumped, financial mess — some consequence he can't blame on anyone else. That's when people go "okay, fine, maybe." Your move is being there when it happens with the offer already on the table.

2. **Trojan horse it.** Not "get assessed for ADHD" but "you keep bitching about X, go find out why, I'll cover it." He walks in about the symptom, the clinician does the rest.

3. **Say it once, straight, and mean it.** "I think you've got ADHD, I think it's costing you, I'll pay for the assessment, offer stands forever." Then never mention it again. The silence after does more work than nagging ever will.

What you don't get to do is force it, and pushing harder just makes him fuse "admitting I need help" with "losing to you." Then even when he privately suspects you're right, he can't act on it without swallowing that. You'd be building the exact wall you're trying to get over.

That's the whole hand. It's just not a hand where you hold the cards.

## Assistant

Right — that I can work with. You're not asking "how do I force him," you're asking "how do I become someone whose push actually lands when the window opens." Different question. Here's the toolkit and why each piece works:

**1. Build the evidence bank.** Start keeping specific receipts — not judgments, events. "You forgot the deposit deadline in March, missed Dara's thing in May, lost the charger argument with your boss in June." Vague ("you're scattered") gets deflected because it's an opinion. Specific gets under the skin because it's just the record, and he has to argue with his own life instead of with you. Three concrete instances beat a hundred "mate, seriously"s.

**2. Learn his actual pain points, not yours.** What YOU see as his ADHD and what HE hates about his life may not overlap. If he doesn't care about being late but hates that he can't finish projects, the projects are your entry point and lateness is worthless as leverage. Influence runs through what he already wants fixed. Find out what that is — usually by listening when he complains, not by observing.

**3. Pre-clear the runway.** Research the clinic, the cost, the wait time, what the assessment actually involves, before you ever need it. The moment of openness — and it comes, usually after a bad week — is short. If your answer to "...okay, how would that even work" is "I'll look into it," the window closes. If it's "there's a place twenty minutes away, one afternoon, I've already covered it, I can book Thursday" — friction zero, pride cost zero.

**4. Stop spending credibility on this topic.** Every time you bring it up unprompted, your next mention is worth less. Nagging trains him to auto-dismiss you. Scarcity is what makes the word land — the mate who says something once a year gets heard; the one who says it monthly is background noise. Bank your credibility now so it has weight later.

**5. Normalize sideways.** Blokes shift on this stuff through proximity, not lectures. A mutual friend who got diagnosed and talks about it casually, a podcast on in the car, an offhand "yeah half of tech has it apparently" — these lower the identity cost of the label without you being the one selling it. You can't be the whole campaign; recruit the environment.

**6. Get your own tell under control.** If you need him to say yes, he'll smell it, and needy pressure reads as agenda. The strongest position is genuine indifference-adjacent calm: offer's there, life goes on either way. That's not giving up — it's what makes the offer feel like a door instead of a trap.

The mechanism underneath all six: you're not building tools to overpower his resistance, you're building tools to make the yes cheap the second his resistance dips on its own. That's the version of "strengthened" that actually converts.
