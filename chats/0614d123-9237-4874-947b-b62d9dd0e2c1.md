# Ideality - Opus 4.8

## User

Analyse in cover mode

## Assistant

Two readings of "Sometimes solutions to non-ideal problems are non-ideal":

**Reading 1: Descriptive — messy problems get messy fixes.**
When the situation itself is compromised, the best available solution inherits the compromise. You can't clean-solve a dirty problem. A drug addict with no support network and no money — the "solution" might be methadone, which is still a drug, still a dependency. Not because you failed to find the elegant answer, but because the elegant answer requires inputs that don't exist. The non-ideal is baked into the constraints, not into your reasoning.

This is basically the engineering version: optimize under the constraints you have, not the ones you wish you had. A pretty solution that ignores the real conditions isn't a solution.

**Reading 2: Normative / political — non-ideal theory.**
This echoes Rawls' distinction between ideal and non-ideal theory. Ideal theory assumes full compliance and favorable conditions; non-ideal theory deals with injustice, partial compliance, scarcity. The claim here: in a world that already violates the ideal, the *right* action can itself be something you'd condemn under ideal conditions. Reparations, affirmative action, civil disobedience, lesser-evil voting — measures that would be unjust or unnecessary in a just world become justified *because* the world isn't just. The non-ideality of the remedy is licensed by the non-ideality of the situation.

The trap in both readings: "non-ideal problems justify non-ideal solutions" is load-bearing and easily abused. It's the same sentence a person uses to excuse a genuinely bad choice ("the situation forced my hand") as the one used to describe a genuinely constrained one. The word doing the work is *sometimes* — it concedes that the inference isn't automatic. The hard part is telling the real constraint from the alibi, and the sentence doesn't help you there.
