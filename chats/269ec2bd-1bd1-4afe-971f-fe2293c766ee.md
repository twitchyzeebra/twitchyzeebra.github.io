# The Mechanisms

Each entry is one mechanism — one weighting, one motion. Not a strength paired with a flaw. The same operation, and the environments that flip its sign. The tuning environment for nearly all of these is the same: small groups, known faces, immediate dangers, scarce calories, information arriving only through direct experience or the mouths of people you knew. Where an entry says "misfit," it means the modern environment broke one of those assumptions.

---

## I. Attention and Perception

**1. Salience weighting.** Attention goes to change, motion, novelty, and threat, in roughly that order. The steady state is processed cheaply or not at all. This is correct when change is where the information is — a rustle in still grass means more than the grass. It fails when the environment learns the weighting and manufactures change on schedule. A news feed is a salience-weighting exploit: it presents novelty at a rate no ancestral environment could, and attention follows the mechanism rather than any actual stakes. The person scrolling is not weak-willed. The mechanism is doing exactly what it does; the input has been shaped to it.

**2. Pattern completion.** Perception and cognition fill gaps from prior structure. You see a whole face from a partial one, hear a whole word through noise, infer a whole motive from two data points. This is the core move of intelligence — prediction is pattern completion forward in time — and it cannot be turned off, only calibrated. It fails when the pattern isn't there: faces in clouds, agency in randomness, plots in coincidence. The insight and the conspiracy theory are the same operation. The only difference is whether the completed pattern exists outside the head, and the mechanism itself contains no check for that. The check has to come from somewhere else — testing, other people, time.

**3. Habituation.** Constant signals fade from awareness. The refrigerator hum disappears; so does the smell of your own house. This is compression — attention is expensive and the unchanging carries no new information. The failure mode is that "unchanging" is judged on the timescale of minutes, so anything that degrades slowly enough stays invisible: a relationship cooling over years, a body deconditioning, a civic norm eroding by increments. Habituation means the most important changes in a life are often the ones structurally hardest to perceive, because they never once produced a detectable difference between today and yesterday.

**4. Attentional narrowing under threat.** Stress collapses the field of attention onto the threat and shortens the time horizon to now. Against a predator this is the right allocation — peripheral considerations genuinely don't matter for the next ten seconds. It fails against modern threats, which are mostly diffuse, slow, and best handled with exactly the wide, long-horizon thinking that the mechanism shuts down. A person in financial trouble narrows onto this week's bill and can't plan the way out; the narrowing is the poverty trap's cognitive component. The mechanism reads chronic stress as one long tiger.

**5. Categorization.** The continuous world gets carved into discrete kinds, and kinds get treated as having essences — an inside that makes the thing what it is. This is what makes learning transferable: know one wolf, know all wolves, and the inference is usually sound because biological kinds do share structure. It fails when applied to categories that don't have essences, which includes most social categories. Stereotyping is not a separate broken faculty; it is kind-inference running on inputs where the kind is shallow. The mechanism cannot distinguish deep categories from constructed ones. It generalizes at the same confidence over both.

---

## II. Memory

**6. Reconstruction.** Memory does not store events; it stores gist and rebuilds detail at recall, using current knowledge as scaffolding. This is enormous compression — a life fits in a head because only the compressed sketch is kept. The cost is that the rebuild is indistinguishable from retrieval from the inside. Confabulated details carry the same felt certainty as accurate ones, and each recall re-saves the reconstruction, so memories drift toward the story you currently tell. Eyewitness confidence and eyewitness accuracy come apart for this reason. There is no flag marking which details were kept and which were filled in.

**7. Decay.** Unrehearsed memories fade. This is retrieval hygiene — a memory system that kept everything at equal strength would drown recall in noise, and the usage-based heuristic (what you haven't needed, you won't need) is usually right. It fails on the important-but-rare: the insight from a book read once, the lesson from a mistake not repeated, the name of the person who mattered but whom you met one time. Importance and frequency-of-use are different quantities, and the mechanism only measures one of them.

**8. Emotional tagging.** High-arousal moments get written deep and stay vivid. Cheap insurance: the events most worth remembering in the ancestral world — attacks, losses, near-misses — were precisely the high-arousal ones, and one-shot permanent learning about them was worth the storage. It fails in two directions. Tagged memories intrude when the tag no longer serves — trauma is emotional tagging without an off-switch. And tagged memories dominate later probability estimates, so a vividly frightening rare event outweighs a boring common one in every subsequent judgment. The mechanism made vividness a proxy for importance, and the proxy broke when vivid events became something other people could manufacture and deliver to you daily.

**9. Hindsight revision.** When the model of the world updates, memory of the old model is quietly overwritten. You do not remember believing what you believed; you remember believing something closer to what you believe now. This keeps the self-model coherent and cheap — maintaining a version history of your own mind would be expensive and mostly useless for action. The cost is that it erases the record of your own learning. "I knew it all along" is not a lie; it's the mechanism working. The practical consequence is that people systematically underestimate how surprising the world is and how often they've been wrong, because the evidence of past wrongness is edited on every update.

## III. Judgment Under Uncertainty

**10. Availability.** Probability is estimated by ease of recall. When your memories are a fair sample of your environment — which they were, when all information arrived through direct experience — this is a legitimate statistical method. It breaks the moment the memory pool is fed by anything other than experience. Media is a firehose of the rare and dramatic, so recall ease now tracks newsworthiness, not frequency, and people fear plane crashes over car trips and strangers over the people they know. The mechanism didn't degrade. Its input assumption — *my memories are my data* — became false.

**11. Anchoring.** The first value encountered becomes the starting point, and adjustment away from it is lazy and insufficient. As a prior-setting strategy this is sound: absent other information, the first number you hear about a quantity really is evidence about it, and in a world where numbers only came from people with knowledge, weighting it heavily worked. It fails when anchors are set by parties with interests — the sticker price, the opening demand, the "was $400" tag — or by pure noise, which the mechanism weights identically. There is no provenance check on the anchor. Anything numeric that arrives first gets the chair.

**12. Prototype matching.** Things are judged by resemblance to the stored typical case. Fast, and usually right — the thing that looks like a duck usually is one. The failure is base-rate neglect: resemblance is computed, prevalence is not. The quiet person who fits the librarian prototype gets classified librarian even in a room that's mostly farmers. The mechanism answers "how much does this look like X?" when the question was "how likely is this to be X?" — and the two answers feel identical from the inside.

**13. Confirmation weighting.** Evidence that fits the current model is processed fluently and accepted; evidence against it triggers scrutiny. This is not a bug bolted onto reasoning — it is how a belief system stays stable enough to act from. A mind that reopened every belief upon every anomaly would never finish a thought; some asymmetric loyalty to the current model is the price of having a model at all. The sign flips with the stakes and the stickiness: on beliefs that are wrong and consequential, the same loyalty becomes a trap that tightens with time, because each survived challenge is counted as further confirmation. Two people running this mechanism honestly on different starting beliefs will diverge forever on the same shared evidence. Polarization needs no villains; it needs only this.

**14. Optimistic miscalibration.** Confidence runs systematically ahead of accuracy, especially about one's own prospects. This looks like pure error until you price the alternative: correctly calibrated beliefs about a hard venture's odds would prevent the venture. The mechanism buys action with distorted maps. Most new businesses fail and most founders know the statistic and start anyway, and civilization is substantially built from the successes of people who were wrong about their odds in the useful direction. The same miscalibration, pointed at a war or a bridge design, kills people. The mechanism can't tell which kind of project it's inflating.

**15. Small-sample learning.** Strong conclusions from two or three instances. Statistically indefensible and evolutionarily mandatory — the organism that needed thirty poisonings to learn about the berry left no descendants. When effects are large and consistent, tiny samples genuinely suffice, and the ancestral environment's important lessons were mostly of that kind. The failure domain is anywhere effects are small, noisy, or probabilistic — medicine, markets, weather, people — where three instances are indistinguishable from chance and the mechanism confidently reads signal anyway. Superstition is small-sample learning applied to noise; so is most of what people believe about what foods, habits, and strangers did to them.

**16. Narrative causality.** Events are stored and understood as stories: agents, motives, consequences, arcs. This is the native compression format for causal knowledge and the only format that transmits reliably between minds — a story survives a thousand retellings; a table of frequencies doesn't survive one. The costs come from the format's requirements. Stories need agents, so agency gets inserted where there is only drift or accident. Stories need single causes, so tangled multi-causal reality gets flattened to a culprit. Stories need arcs, so randomness gets read as rise and fall, punishment and desert. The just-world belief — that outcomes track what people deserve — is narrative causality applied to the whole of life, and it is comforting in exactly the way that makes it cruel to the unlucky.

**17. Closure seeking.** Open questions cost. The mind wants a verdict — any verdict — so that action can proceed and the question can be released. Under time pressure this is right: a decent answer now beats a perfect answer after the moment has passed, and most ancestral questions were time-pressured. The failure is that the discomfort of open questions doesn't scale with the stakes of closing them wrongly. The mechanism pays the same relief for a premature verdict on a trivial matter and on a defining one. Ideology, at the mechanical level, is bulk-purchased closure: one framework that settles every question in advance, so that nothing ever has to be held open again. The tranquility is real. So is the price.

## IV. Valuation and Motivation

**18. Loss aversion.** Losses are weighted roughly twice as heavily as equal gains. This is correct arithmetic near irreversibility: at the margin of survival, the downside of a loss (death, injury, ruin) is unbounded in a way the upside of an equivalent gain is not, so asymmetric weighting is not a bias there — it's the right answer. The sign flips in any domain where losses are survivable and recoverable, which is most of modern life. There it produces the endowment effect, the sunk-cost trap, the career not left, the position not sold, the status quo defended past its usefulness. One weighting, correct near cliffs, expensive on flat ground — and the mechanism has no altimeter.

**19. Present bias.** Rewards now are worth disproportionately more than rewards later, with the discount curve bent hard at the near end. This was rational under the conditions that built it: an uncertain future, no storage, no enforcement of promises. A bird in hand genuinely was worth two in a bush that might be gone tomorrow, along with you. The modern environment — refrigeration, contracts, compound interest, antibiotics — made the future reliable enough that the old discount rate became a tax. Every under-funded retirement and abandoned exercise plan is the mechanism pricing the future at ancestral risk levels in a world that lowered them.

**20. Hedonic adaptation.** The baseline resets. Gains stop registering; the new house becomes just the house; the raise becomes the salary. This looks like a curse and is half of one, but it's the same mechanism that resets after losses — grief that would otherwise be permanent becomes survivable, disability becomes livable, and people rebuilding from ruin find, to their own surprise, ordinary days again. Adaptation is what keeps the motivational gradient alive at any absolute level: an organism permanently satisfied by past gains stops moving. The cost is the treadmill — acquisition aimed at a lasting shift in feeling is aimed at something the mechanism is specifically built to prevent. It resets in both directions and asks no permission for either.

**21. Effort discounting.** Actions are priced by their energy cost, and the pricing is stingy. In a calorie-scarce world this was accounting, not vice — wasted effort was a survival expense, and the organism that lazed efficiently outlived the one that toiled indiscriminately. The same stinginess drives tool-making, shortcut-finding, and most efficiency gains in history: laziness with an engineering degree. It fails in calorie abundance, where the conserved energy has nowhere to go and the mechanism still vetoes the walk, the draft, the hard conversation, pricing them in a currency that stopped being scarce. Procrastination is a functioning frugality module that outlived its famine.

**22. Curiosity.** Information carries intrinsic reward, separate from any use. This is the exploration budget — an organism that only sought information with known payoff would never find the payoffs it didn't know about, so the mechanism pays for looking as such. It made the species. Its failure mode is any environment that decouples the reward from the value: gossip, trivia, feeds, and news cycles deliver the information-shaped reward at industrial rates with none of the payload. Doomscrolling is curiosity metabolizing empty calories. The mechanism counts novelty in, not value out, because in its home environment the two were correlated. They aren't anymore.

**23. Disgust.** A hard rejection response tuned to pathogen cues — decay, contamination, bodily fluids — with the signature property that contact contaminates: the fly makes the whole soup inedible. As pathogen defense this is well-calibrated; germ theory largely vindicated its intuitions millennia early. The trouble is that the mechanism accepts moral and social inputs and processes them with the same physics. Ideas become contaminating, people become unclean, contact with the out-group becomes pollution. Every purge and pogrom recruits disgust vocabulary — vermin, filth, disease — because the mechanism, once pointed at humans, delivers precisely the response the pointing intended: rejection without deliberation, and contamination by mere association. It is the most weaponizable mechanism on this list.

**24. Fear generalization.** One bad encounter propagates caution across everything resembling it. The asymmetry is deliberate: over-generalizing costs some foregone berries; under-generalizing costs your life. Insurance priced accordingly. The failure is that the generalization has no natural stopping radius and no expiration. One dog bite becomes all dogs, becomes the street with dogs, becomes going outside. Anxiety disorders are fear generalization compounding without the corrective re-exposures that a rougher world used to force. The mechanism was tuned for organisms that couldn't afford to avoid everything and would therefore be re-tested against their fears whether they chose it or not. Modern life makes total avoidance affordable, which removes the only brake the mechanism ever had.

## V. Social Cognition

**25. Intention attribution.** Behavior is explained by inner states — beliefs, desires, plans. This is the most sophisticated thing the species does: modeling minds allows cooperation, deception, teaching, negotiation, and the entire social world. It runs so cheaply and automatically that it applies to everything: the car that "doesn't want" to start, the weather that punishes, the dice that owe you. Anthropomorphism, animism, and paranoia are all over-application — the mechanism seeing minds where there are none, or seeing more scheming in a real mind than exists. The paranoid reading is favored under threat for the same insurance logic as fear generalization: modeling a hostile mind that isn't there is cheaper than missing one that is. Most perceived slights are this asymmetry, not actual malice.

**26. Reciprocity tracking.** A running ledger of favors given and received, per person, with strong emotions enforcing the balance — gratitude when owed to, obligation when owing, anger when stiffed. This ledger is the original credit system; it allows exchange across time with no contracts and no currency, and it built every human economy before the first coin. Its failures: the ledger never closes, so grudges compound across decades; and it can be exploited by anyone who understands that an unsolicited gift creates real debt — the free sample, the favor you didn't ask for, the lavish gesture that arrives with an invisible invoice. The mechanism cannot decline an entry.

**27. Reputation sensitivity.** Behavior shifts under observation, toward the observable virtues. Cynically described as hypocrisy, but it is the enforcement layer of cooperation: in a group where everyone knows everyone, being watched is the normal condition, and the mechanism makes prosociality the default posture. The failures are the two edges. Under anonymity — cities, cars, comment sections — the mechanism disengages and behavior degrades, not because character changed but because the input signal went dark. And under performance conditions, the mechanism optimizes the appearance of virtue as a separate product from virtue itself, which is where moral display detaches from moral behavior. Both failures are the same mechanism reading the audience correctly.

**28. In-group preference.** Trust, generosity, and benefit-of-the-doubt concentrate on the group; scrutiny concentrates outside it. This is what makes high-trust cooperation possible at all — you cannot extend full-price trust to everyone and survive contact with the few who exploit it, so trust must be budgeted, and the group boundary is the budget line. Everything good about tight communities runs on this concentration. The cost is the gradient itself: the same mechanism that produces solidarity inside produces indifference and then dehumanization outside, and the boundary is arbitrary — the minimal-group experiments showed that sorting people by coin flip suffices to activate it. The mechanism needs a boundary and does not care where it gets one.

**29. Conformity.** When uncertain, copy the majority. This is rational statistics dressed as weakness: the local majority's behavior encodes generations of trial and error about local conditions, and the copier inherits the answers without paying for the errors. Don't eat what nobody eats — the reasons are real even when nobody remembers them. The failure is the information cascade: when everyone is copying, the majority's behavior stops encoding evidence and starts encoding only itself, and the whole group can march confidently on the accumulated weight of nobody's knowledge. Bubbles, panics, and the bystander frozen in a crowd of frozen bystanders are conformity consuming its own output as input.

**30. Prestige-biased learning.** Copy the successful, specifically. This is the refinement that made cumulative culture possible — copying the best hunter transmits skill faster than copying the average one, and ranking models by outcome is sound. The failure is in what gets copied: the mechanism can't isolate which of the successful person's traits caused the success, so it copies the bundle — the technique along with the diet, the superstitions, the shoes. Celebrity endorsement is a direct commercial tap on this mechanism: prestige earned in one domain transferring, mechanically and irrelevantly, to products in another. Cargo cults are the same copying error at civilizational scale.

**31. Fairness enforcement.** Exploitation triggers anger and a willingness to punish at personal cost. Rejecting an unfair split — burning your own share to burn the exploiter's — is irrational in the single round and load-bearing across all rounds: cooperation survives only where cheating is expensive, and the mechanism supplies the expense, voluntarily, forever, without needing courts. Its failures are spite — the same costly punishment fired at offenses too small to merit it, or continued past any deterrent value — and the blocking of positive-sum deals that are unequal but good, where everyone gains and the mechanism still says no because someone gains *more*. It measures the split, not the size of the pie.

**32. Empathy.** Others' states are simulated in one's own — their pain runs on your pain hardware. This is the direct motivational source of care, and it scales beautifully to one and terribly to many. The simulator is single-threaded: one identified child, vividly rendered, engages it fully; a million children, delivered as a statistic, engage it not at all — and the difference between the two responses is not a moral judgment, it's a rendering limitation. Compassion fade is the honest output of a mechanism doing per-person simulation being handed aggregates. The same limitation makes empathy steerable: whoever controls which single face you see controls where the care goes, which is why every cause and every war leads with one child's photograph.

## VI. The Self-Model

**33. Self-serving attribution.** Successes are credited to the self; failures to circumstance. This looks like simple vanity and functions as motivational maintenance: an agent that internalized every failure fully would rationally stop attempting things, and depression's attributional style — failures internal and permanent, successes external and fluke — shows what the mechanism looks like switched off. The cost is symmetric: the same attribution that protects the will to act protects errors from correction, since a failure that was circumstance's fault carries no lesson. The mechanism keeps you moving and unteachable in the same motion.

**34. Dissonance reduction.** When beliefs and actions conflict, the beliefs move — actions are already spent. This is identity maintenance: an agent whose self-model diverged too far from its behavior couldn't predict itself, and self-prediction is what commitment is made of. The cost is direction: it means past actions justify themselves retroactively. The hazing survivor values the group *because* the hazing was costly; the sunk investment gets a rosier forecast *because* it was made; the escalating war gets nobler *because* of the dead so far. Each action becomes evidence for the belief that would justify it, and the loop runs on any behavior at all, including the worst ones.

**35. Confabulated agency.** The reasons you give for your choices are largely composed after the choice, by a narrator with no access to the actual machinery. The split-brain experiments showed this starkly — the narrating hemisphere fluently explaining choices it demonstrably didn't make, with total sincerity — but the narrator runs in everyone, all the time. Its function is real: a coherent first-person story makes the self predictable, plannable, and explainable to others, and those are the load-bearing properties. The cost is that introspection feels like inspection and is actually authorship. "I know why I did that" is almost always a well-written guess, and the confidence attached to it is the narrator's confidence in the prose, not access to the cause.

**36. Social comparison.** Standing is computed relative to a reference group, not on any absolute scale. This is the ambition calibrator: absolute measures of "doing well" don't exist in nature, so the local group serves as the yardstick, and comparing to slightly-better neighbors generates exactly the achievable, motivating gap that pulls performance upward. The mechanism assumed the reference group would be a village — people of roughly your circumstances, whose lives you saw whole, drudgery included. The modern reference group is a curated global feed of everyone's best moment. Against that yardstick, every actual life measures poor, and the mechanism dutifully reports failure amid material conditions kings didn't have. The misery is not confusion. It is a correct reading of a rigged comparison.

## VII. Learning and Reward

**37. Prediction-error learning.** The reward system pays out not for good outcomes but for outcomes *better than predicted* — the surprise is the signal, and fully expected rewards go quiet. This is the correct learning algorithm; it's the same one that trains artificial networks, and it concentrates learning exactly where the model is wrong, which is the only place learning is needed. Its failure is structural and was found by casinos before it was found by neuroscientists: variable, unpredictable rewards never stop generating error, so they never stop generating signal. The slot machine, the loot box, and the pull-to-refresh feed are prediction-error generators — machines for manufacturing perpetual surprise — and addiction to them is the learning algorithm doing flawless credit assignment on an environment built to make the credit never resolve.

**38. Play.** Simulation of serious situations with the stakes removed — fighting without injury, chasing without predation, pretending without commitment. This is the cheapest training regime that exists, and every intelligent species runs it. Rehearsal at zero cost, exploration of the possibility space around real skills, and in humans it extends into imagination, fiction, and hypothesis — the whole capacity to think about what isn't. Its failure mode is the inverted case: environments that deliver the *feeling* of skill-rehearsal with no transferable skill — play signals decoupled from the competence play evolved to build. The mechanism can't audit whether the practice is practice for anything.

## VIII. One Closing Row

**39. Compression-before-survey.** The mind claims a territory in one line before walking it — writes "every mechanism, every flaw" on a folder and feels the shape of the whole project in the label. This is how anything too large to justify gets started: the compression creates the workspace, and no genome gets mapped and no catalog gets built without someone first writing down a scope they couldn't yet fill. It fails when the label satisfies the appetite it was supposed to organize — when the sentence, being grammatically complete, gets filed as work complete.

## I. Attention and Perception

**1. Salience weighting.**
Working: A driver on a monotonous highway snaps alert the instant a deer's shape appears at the tree line — hours of unchanging road processed at near-zero cost, the one change caught immediately. A parent asleep through traffic noise wakes to their infant's cough.
Misfiring: The average person checks their phone dozens of times a day, largely because notifications are engineered as change-events — red badges, motion, sound. Local TV news leads with the night's one shooting rather than the ten thousand uneventful commutes, and viewers' sense of their city's danger tracks the broadcast, not the crime statistics.

**2. Pattern completion.**
Working: A radiologist glances at a scan and sees the anomaly before consciously searching — thousands of prior images completing the pattern. You understand speech at a loud party despite hearing maybe 70% of the phonemes.
Misfiring: The "Face on Mars" — a 1976 Viking photo of a mesa that half the public read as an artificial monument, until higher-resolution images showed a hill. Gamblers seeing "hot" roulette wheels. A person in early psychosis reading a stranger's cough as a signal about them — same completion engine, no external pattern.

**3. Habituation.**
Working: People living near train tracks stop hearing trains within weeks and sleep fine. A surgeon's first incision is overwhelming; the five-hundredth is workable — the arousal that would impair skill has faded.
Misfiring: A couple realizes in year twelve that they haven't had a real conversation in years — no single day differed enough from the previous one to register. A person gains sixty pounds a half-pound at a time and is genuinely startled by an old photo. Employees at a firm with slowly-corroding ethics later can't identify when it went bad, because it never went bad on any particular Tuesday.

**4. Attentional narrowing under threat.**
Working: A pedestrian jumps back from a bus and only afterward reconstructs having seen it — the narrowing acted before deliberation could. Firefighters describe tunnel focus during rescues: the burning room, the person, nothing else.
Misfiring: The documented finding that financial scarcity consumes cognitive bandwidth — sugarcane farmers in one well-known study performed measurably worse on reasoning tests before harvest (broke) than after (paid), same farmers, same tests. A student panicking over one failing course stops attending the others. Test anxiety: the threat is the exam, the narrowing shuts down exactly the broad recall the exam requires.

**5. Categorization.**
Working: A toddler shown one dog generalizes correctly to all dogs — different sizes, colors, breeds — within a handful of examples, something machine vision needed millions of images to approximate. A forager taught that one mushroom species kills avoids the whole species, correctly, forever.
Misfiring: A hiring manager reads two resumes identical except for the names — "Emily" and "Lakisha," in the famous audit study — and calls back Emily 50% more often. The category fired; the essence it inferred isn't there. Any sentence beginning "people from that country are..."

## II. Memory

**6. Reconstruction.**
Working: You can describe your childhood kitchen well enough to navigate it, without having stored a photograph of it — gist plus reconstruction, at a millionth the storage cost.
Misfiring: The "lost in the mall" experiments — researchers implanted entirely false childhood memories in about a quarter of adult subjects, who then confidently supplied details no one had given them. Over 300 U.S. convictions overturned by DNA evidence involved eyewitnesses who were certain. After 9/11, researchers collected people's memories of hearing the news within days, then re-asked years later: the accounts had changed substantially, and confidence had gone *up*.

**7. Decay.**
Working: You don't remember what you had for lunch on March 4th of last year, and nothing about your life is worse for it. Old phone numbers fade once replaced, keeping the current one retrievable without interference.
Misfiring: You read a book that genuinely changed your thinking, and eighteen months later can recall the fact that it was important but not one specific idea from it. The name of the ER doctor who saved your parent — gone, because you only heard it once, while "Wednesday is trash day" is permanent through sheer repetition.

**8. Emotional tagging.**
Working: A child touches a hot stove once. One trial, permanent, correct. You remember exactly where you were during the worst phone call of your life, which is useful for processing what followed.
Misfiring: A veteran hears a car backfire and is briefly *in* Fallujah — the tag firing at full intensity a decade past its usefulness. After the movie *Jaws*, beach attendance dropped measurably; annual shark deaths worldwide are single digits, while the drive to the beach kills thousands. People who watched the 2008 crash on the news at an impressionable age avoiding stocks for life, at enormous compounding cost.

**9. Hindsight revision.**
Working: A chess player who has improved doesn't clutter their play with vivid memories of how the board looked to their weaker self; the old, wrong evaluations are simply overwritten.
Misfiring: Ask people in 2021 whether they saw the pandemic coming in January 2020 — a striking number remember early alarm that their own texts and tweets from the time contradict. Analysts after every election explaining that the result was obvious, when their published forecasts said otherwise. The classic study: people told the outcome of a historical battle rated that outcome as having been predictable all along, unlike people not told.

## III. Judgment Under Uncertainty

**10. Availability.**
Working: A farmer estimates how often the creek floods from memory of past floods and gets it about right — his memories genuinely are a fair sample of the creek.
Misfiring: In the year after 9/11, enough Americans switched from planes to cars that researchers attributed roughly 1,500 additional road deaths to the substitution — fleeing a vivid risk into a statistically larger one. Parents forbid unsupervised outdoor play over stranger abduction (vanishingly rare, heavily televised) while driving those children daily (leading cause of child death). People buy earthquake insurance in the months after an earthquake — when the risk is, if anything, lower.

**11. Anchoring.**
Working: A contractor asked to estimate an unfamiliar job starts from the last similar job's cost and adjusts — the anchor is real information and the estimate lands close.
Misfiring: The experiment where subjects spun a rigged wheel of fortune, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the UN — those who spun 65 guessed higher than those who spun 10, anchored on a number they had watched being generated randomly. Every "was $499, now $249" tag. Experienced judges in one German study gave longer sentences after rolling loaded dice that showed high numbers.

**12. Prototype matching.**
Working: A veteran nurse looks at a patient the monitors say is fine and says "he doesn't look right" hours before the crash — pattern-match against thousands of prior patients, faster than any explicit rule.
Misfiring: The original Linda problem: given a description of a socially conscious philosophy graduate, most people rate "bank teller and feminist" as more probable than "bank teller" — logically impossible, but the prototype fits better. A 55-year-old woman's heart attack gets misdiagnosed as anxiety at higher rates than a man's, because the stored heart-attack prototype clutches a male chest.

**13. Confirmation weighting.**
Working: A physicist doesn't reopen conservation of energy every time a lab result seems to violate it; she assumes equipment error and is almost always right. Stable science requires exactly this loyalty to established models.
Misfiring: In studies, subjects shown the same mixed evidence on capital punishment came away *more* convinced of their prior position — both sides, same data. A person who believes their partner is cheating reads every late arrival as proof and every kindness as cover. Two cable news audiences watching the same event and each seeing it confirm their frame, nightly, for decades.

**14. Optimistic miscalibration.**
Working: Roughly half of new restaurants fail within five years; nearly every founder knows this; the restaurants open anyway, and the ones that survive are why your city has food. Patients with unrealistically hopeful prognosis beliefs sometimes show better treatment adherence — the false map produces the real behavior.
Misfiring: The Sydney Opera House: estimated at $7 million and four years; delivered at $102 million and fourteen. Nearly every megaproject overruns, in the same direction, forever — the planning fallacy is the most replicated finding in forecasting. In 1914, all sides expected the war over by Christmas.

**15. Small-sample learning.**
Working: You eat at a new place, spend the night sick, and never go back. One data point, and correctly so — the cost of more data is more nights like that. A child grabs one wasp.
Misfiring: A grandfather smoked until 94, so cigarettes get a family pass. An athlete wears the socks from the winning game for the rest of the season. A tourist is treated rudely twice in Paris and knows what the French are like. A manager interviews a candidate for thirty minutes and confidently overrides a work history spanning fifteen years.

**16. Narrative causality.**
Working: "Grandfather planted too early one spring, the frost took everything, and the family nearly starved" — transmits a planting rule across generations more durably than any almanac table. Aesop's fables are load-bearing risk education that survived 2,500 years of retelling.
Misfiring: A pandemic emerges and, within weeks, large populations require a villain — a lab, a bioweapon, a plot — because "spillover happens stochastically" is not a story. Financial journalism explaining every daily market move with a cause ("stocks fell on inflation fears") when most daily movement is noise. Telling a cancer patient everything happens for a reason.

**17. Closure seeking.**
Working: An ER physician with a crashing patient commits to a working diagnosis in ninety seconds and treats — an open mind is a luxury the patient can't fund. A general who waits for certainty loses to one who acts on adequacy.
Misfiring: A detective settles early on a suspect and every subsequent finding is bent to fit — a pattern documented across wrongful-conviction cases. A grieving family accepts a tidy but wrong explanation because "we'll never really know" is unbearable. The convert who, after years of exhausting doubt, adopts a total ideology and describes the moment as *relief* — every open question closed at once, wholesale.

## IV. Valuation and Motivation

**18. Loss aversion.**
Working: A mountaineer 300 meters from the summit turns around because the weather window is closing — weighting the loss (death) far above the gain (summit) is not bias up there, it's the correct math. Most who died on Everest died in the pursuit of the gain.
Misfiring: The endowment experiments — students given a mug demand roughly twice as much to sell it as identical students will pay to buy it, ownership alone doubling the value. Investors holding collapsing stock for years because selling makes the loss real. Staying seven years in the wrong job, marriage, or city because leaving registers as loss and staying registers as nothing.

**19. Present bias.**
Working: A soldier on leave before deployment spends the money and takes the trip — steep discounting of an uncertain future is correct when the future is genuinely uncertain.
Misfiring: The marshmallow, obviously — but more consequentially: automatic-enrollment pension schemes raised participation from roughly 60% to over 90% by changing nothing except which choice required effort *now*. Half-finished antibiotic courses, because the sickness is gone today and resistance is next year's problem. The smoker's arithmetic, where each individual cigarette costs approximately nothing.

**20. Hedonic adaptation.**
Working: The foundational study: paraplegic accident victims, interviewed a year on, reported day-to-day moods far closer to normal than anyone predicts — including them, beforehand. Widows and widowers mostly return to their baseline. This is the mechanism as mercy.
Misfiring: The same study's other arm: lottery winners, a year later, were not happier than controls and took *less* pleasure in ordinary things. The raise that felt life-changing in March is invisible by September, and the new number is now the baseline against which the next raise will be judged. The dream house becomes the house with the gutters that need doing.

**21. Effort discounting.**
Working: The wheel, the plow, the dishwasher, the keyboard shortcut — nearly every efficiency in civilization is effort-discounting given tools. The farmer who invented a lazier way to irrigate fed more people.
Misfiring: A person drives around a parking lot for five minutes to avoid a two-minute walk. Gym memberships: the industry's revenue model is built on the measured gap between January's intention and February's attendance. The email that would take four minutes to write, deferred daily for three weeks at a total anxiety cost dwarfing the four minutes.

**22. Curiosity.**
Working: Fleming notices the mold that killed his bacteria culture and, instead of binning the ruined plate, wonders. Penicillin. A toddler puts everything in its mouth and, in eighteen months, builds a working physics of its environment.
Misfiring: A person opens their phone to check the weather and surfaces forty minutes later knowing what a stranger said about another stranger's wedding. Clickbait headlines are curiosity arbitrage — "you won't believe what happened next" is a payload-free information-shaped object. Knowing celebrity trivia at industrial depth while one's own tax situation remains a mystery.

**23. Disgust.**
Working: Milk smells off and you don't drink it — a chemistry lab in the nose, correct millennia before germ theory. Cultures with heavy meat spicing in hot climates: disgust-driven cuisine as pathogen control.
Misfiring: Rwanda, 1994: the radio called Tutsis *inyenzi* — cockroaches — for months before the machetes. Nazi propaganda cut film of Jews with film of rats. In experiments, people refuse to drink juice a sterilized cockroach has touched, and refuse to wear a killer's laundered sweater — contamination physics applied where no contaminant exists. The same transfer runs every "those people are dirty" politics in history.

**24. Fear generalization.**
Working: A child bitten by one snake fears all snakes and their fear costs them nothing but a wide path through tall grass — insurance priced correctly.
Misfiring: A person has one panic attack in a supermarket. They avoid that supermarket, then all supermarkets, then crowds, then leaving the house — agoraphobia's standard progression, each avoidance preventing the disconfirming re-exposure that would have stopped the spread. A generation of parents, generalizing from televised abductions, raises the first children in history with no unsupervised outdoor range, and anxiety rates in those children set records.

## V. Social Cognition

**25. Intention attribution.**
Working: A poker player reads a bet's *meaning* — what does he want me to believe he has? — three levels of nested mind-modeling, running in seconds. A parent knows from the specific quality of the silence upstairs that the toddler is doing something forbidden.
Misfiring: The 1944 Heider-Simmel experiment: subjects shown animated triangles and circles moving around a screen spontaneously described bullying, fear, chasing, rescue — full minds attributed to geometry. People name their cars and apologize to Roombas. And the dark version: a driver who fails to signal a lane change "cut you off *deliberately*," and the road rage that follows is intention attribution's insurance asymmetry choosing the hostile read.

**26. Reciprocity tracking.**
Working: Neighbors who have traded favors for a decade — tools, casseroles, airport rides — with no ledger written anywhere and both parties knowing the balance to the ounce. This system underwrote all human commerce before money.
Misfiring: The Hare Krishna airport technique, studied and named: press a flower into a traveler's hand as a "gift," and donation rates jump — many recipients visibly resent the flower, throw it away around the corner, and donate anyway. The debt was entered involuntarily. Pharmaceutical companies learned that a pizza lunch measurably shifts physicians' prescribing. Family feuds where the original unpaid entry is two generations old and no living person can state it.

**27. Reputation sensitivity.**
Working: The honesty-box studies: a poster of a pair of eyes above an unattended coffee-payment tin roughly tripled contributions versus a poster of flowers. Watched — even by paper eyes — people are better. Small towns run on this.
Misfiring: The anonymity edge: the same mild-mannered commuter who apologizes for brushing your sleeve becomes, in a comment section under a pseudonym, someone their family wouldn't recognize. The performance edge: a corporation spends more publicizing its charitable giving than it gave; the mechanism was optimizing the visible signal, and the signal detached from its referent entirely.

**28. In-group preference.**
Working: A Sikh temple's langar kitchen feeds thousands weekly on volunteer labor and donations — trust concentrated inside a community boundary producing cooperation that no contract could buy. Immigrant enclaves lending each other business capital no bank would extend.
Misfiring: Tajfel's minimal group experiments: boys sorted by a coin flip — told only "you're group A" — immediately allocated more money to anonymous in-group members and would *sacrifice their own group's total* to widen the gap over group B. A coin flip. Sports riots. And every ethnic conflict, which is langar-kitchen solidarity with the gradient's outer slope pointed at neighbors.

**29. Conformity.**
Working: A tourist in a new city eats where the locals queue and avoids the empty restaurant next door — inheriting the verdict of a thousand meals they didn't have to eat. You don't drink the water nobody drinks; the cholera that taught that lesson is long forgotten and the lesson still holds.
Misfiring: Asch, 1951: seated among confederates who all confidently gave the same wrong answer about which printed line was longer, a third of subjects gave the wrong answer too — denying their own eyes. Kitty Genovese-style bystander freezes: each onlooker reads the others' inaction as evidence there's no emergency, a crowd of people copying each other's copying. The 2008 housing bubble: everyone's confidence sourced from everyone's confidence.

**30. Prestige-biased learning.**
Working: An apprentice chooses to train under the region's best luthier rather than the nearest one — ranking models by outcome, and inheriting technique it took the master forty years to refine.
Misfiring: Michael Jordan sold underwear, hot dogs, and cologne — prestige earned at basketball transferring to products his skill has no bearing on, at a price premium millions paid. Melanesian cargo cults after WWII built bamboo control towers and coconut headphones — copying the visible bundle of behaviors that preceded cargo, unable to isolate which elements caused it. Every founder who adopted Steve Jobs's turtleneck and cruelty but not his taste.

**31. Fairness enforcement.**
Working: The ultimatum game, run worldwide: offered $2 of a $10 split, most people reject it — burning their own $2 to burn the proposer's $8 — and *because* everyone knows people do this, proposers offer fair splits. The threat of costly punishment is why the threat rarely executes. Whistleblowers who end their careers to end a fraud.
Misfiring: A divorce where both parties knowingly spend the estate on lawyers to keep the other from getting it. Capuchin monkeys in de Waal's experiment hurling perfectly good cucumber at the researcher upon seeing the neighbor get grapes — the cucumber was fine yesterday; the *split* changed. Nations rejecting trade deals that would enrich them because the partner gains more.

**32. Empathy.**
Working: A stranger falls on the subway platform and four people are moving before thinking — the simulation of his pain fired in their bodies faster than deliberation. Organ donations to identified individuals.
Misfiring: The documented "identifiable victim effect": donations for one named, photographed child exceed donations for a statistical eight children, and in the starkest version, *adding statistics to the child's story lowers giving*. Three-year-old Alan Kurdi's photograph on a Turkish beach moved more refugee policy in a week than four years of casualty figures — one rendered face versus 400,000 unrendered ones. The mechanism is why charities show you one child and why war propaganda shows you one atrocity: the steering wheel is the face, and whoever picks the face steers.

## VI. The Self-Model

**33. Self-serving attribution.**
Working: A salesman goes 0-for-30 on cold calls, attributes it to a bad list and a bad week rather than to being fundamentally unpersuasive, and makes 30 more calls Monday. The attribution is probably partly false and entirely necessary; call 31 doesn't happen without it.
Misfiring: Surveyed drivers rating themselves — depending on the study, 80-93% place themselves above the median. A student aces one exam ("I'm good at this") and fails the next ("the professor's tests are unfair"), extracting a lesson from neither. Executives attributing the boom years to strategy and the bust years to macroeconomic headwinds, in consecutive annual reports.

**34. Dissonance reduction.**
Working: A person who volunteers once, on a whim, updates their self-model to "I'm someone who volunteers" and then keeps volunteering to match it — the behavior recruited the belief, and the loop, pointed this direction, builds character from accidents.
Misfiring: Festinger's original: subjects paid $1 to tell the next person a tedious task was fun later rated the task as genuinely more enjoyable than subjects paid $20 — the $20 group had a sufficient external reason for lying; the $1 group had to *believe it*. Fraternity hazing producing fiercer loyalty than open admission — the suffering must have been for something. The doomsday cult Festinger infiltrated: when the world failed to end on schedule, the members *proselytized harder*. And every war's middle years, when the dead so far become the reason for the dead to come.

**35. Confabulated agency.**
Working: "I took the job because I wanted more responsibility" — probably composed after a decision made by machinery the speaker can't inspect, but the story lets them make coherent future choices, and lets their spouse plan around a predictable person. The narration is functional even when it's fiction.
Misfiring: Gazzaniga's split-brain patients: the right hemisphere shown "walk," the patient stands, and the left hemisphere — asked why — says fluently, "I'm going to get a Coke," with zero awareness of fabricating. In the shopping study, customers chose among *identical* stockings, showed a strong position bias (rightmost wins), and every one of them explained their choice by texture and quality; told about the position effect, they denied it applied to them. Everyone's account of why they married who they married.

**36. Social comparison.**
Working: A middle-of-the-pack runner joins a club where most members are slightly faster and, within a year, is faster — the visible, achievable gap did what no training plan alone does. Olympic finals produce world records; time trials don't.
Misfiring: The bronze/silver finding: bronze medalists are measurably happier on the podium than silver medalists — bronze compares down to fourth place, silver compares up to gold. The study result that a majority of people would prefer earning $50k where others earn $25k over earning $100k where others earn $200k — sacrificing half their absolute income to win the local comparison. And Instagram, which is a machine for making everyone the silver medalist: multiple internal and academic studies linking teen-girl feed exposure to body-image collapse, against a reference group of professionally lit strangers' best single frames.

## VII. Learning and Reward

**37. Prediction-error learning.**
Working: A jazz student's teacher plays a chord substitution the student didn't see coming, and *that* voicing — the surprising one — is the one burned in by nightfall. Expected material slides off; violation teaches. All effective teaching engineers surprise.
Misfiring: B.F. Skinner's pigeons, then Las Vegas's engineers, then Silicon Valley's: rewards on a *variable* schedule produce responding that never extinguishes, because the prediction error never resolves. Slot machines take more money than casinos' table games precisely because they are purer error generators. Pull-to-refresh was consciously modeled on the slot machine lever, per its own designers' later regrets. A gambling addict at a machine is not weak; they are hosting the correct learning algorithm inside a building constructed so that learning can never complete.

**38. Play.**
Working: Wolf pups play-fighting are calibrating bite inhibition and combat skill at zero mortality — the cubs that play more fight better as adults. Flight simulators. A child playing house is rehearsing social roles two decades in advance. Einstein's "thought experiments" — riding a light beam — are play with the stakes removed from physics.
Misfiring: A player logs 3,000 hours in a game whose skill curve is engineered to *feel* like mastery-building while transferring nothing — the rehearsal reward without the rehearsal. Contrast a flight sim (play that builds the real skill) with a slot-machine-mechanics mobile game wearing a strategy costume: the mechanism can't audit the difference, and the industry's revenue depends on it not being able to.

## VIII. Closing Row

**39. Compression-before-survey.**
Working: Kennedy, 1961: "land a man on the moon before this decade is out" — a scope claim no one could yet fill, which created the workspace that 400,000 people then filled. Darwin's notebook "B," begun with one line about species change, decades before he could cash it. Every dissertation begins as a one-line lie about its own tractability.
Misfiring: The domain name bought, the folder labeled, the gym joined. Notebooks full of first pages. A note that says "every flaw and strength of every core human mental mechanism" — filed, felt as finished, and reopened only because someone asked twice.
