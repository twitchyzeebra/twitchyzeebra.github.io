## Assistant

**"Facebook addict is the lowest class of human"**

This is a status claim disguised as a media critique. Nobody who says this is actually worried about screen time — they're drawing a class line and putting themselves on the right side of it.

**What the statement is doing:**

Digital platform use has a prestige hierarchy, and everyone online knows it intuitively even if nobody writes it down. Facebook sits at the bottom of that hierarchy — not because it's the most addictive (TikTok wins that contest easily), but because of *who's imagined to be on it*: aging relatives sharing minion memes, hoax warnings, AI-generated shrimp Jesus, marketplace scammers, and people arguing in local community groups. "Facebook addict" doesn't conjure a tech-compulsion victim; it conjures a *type* — older, less educated, less media-literate, provincial. The statement is punching at that type.

Compare: nobody says "Letterboxd addict is the lowest class of human." Same compulsive checking behavior, different imagined user, different verdict. That asymmetry is the tell. The claim isn't about addiction — addiction is the socially acceptable wrapper for contempt that's really about class, age, and taste.

**Where this lives in the discourse:**

- **The dead-platform sneer.** Facebook-as-punchline has been stable internet culture since roughly 2015, when young users fled and it became "the boomer platform." Saying you're still on Facebook is a confession; being *addicted* to it is the confession squared.
- **Addiction-as-insult.** "Addict" here isn't clinical, it's moral. The internet uses addiction language to mean *weak-willed* — someone who consumes low-status content and can't stop, which doubles the shame.
- **The irony trap.** These statements are almost always posted *on another platform*, by someone compulsively using *that* platform. The Reddit user dunking on Facebook addicts at 2am is the joke telling itself.

**The response it generates:**

Agreement from people who want the status boost, pushback of the "let people enjoy things" variety, and occasionally the sharper reply: that ranking humans by their coping mechanisms is a worse look than the doomscrolling. The "lowest class of human" framing is doing a lot of work — it's hyperbole as bonding ritual, the kind of statement that isn't meant to be defended, just co-signed.

**The honest read:** it's snobbery wearing concern's clothes. The interesting question it accidentally raises — why we assign moral rank to *which* algorithm eats someone's attention rather than *that* one does — goes unasked, because asking it would collapse the hierarchy the speaker is standing on.
