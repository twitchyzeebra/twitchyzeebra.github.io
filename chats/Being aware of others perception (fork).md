# How You're Perceived: A Field Guide
*For people who want the information without the panic attack that usually comes bundled with it.*

---

## Why the question hurts in the first place

"How do people see me?" gets processed by the same brain machinery that once tracked whether the tribe was about to leave you behind. Social evaluation is filed under survival, so the brain doesn't answer the question neutrally — it answers with the worst plausible case, because for most of human history, underestimating rejection got you killed and overestimating it just made you cautious. Your anxiety is a smoke detector calibrated to go off for toast.

This means the raw feeling you get when you wonder how you came across is not information about how you came across. It's information about your threat system. Two different channels. Everything below is about reading the first channel without tripping the second.

## The thumbnail principle

People do not perceive you. They perceive a compressed thumbnail built from three or four data points — how you greeted them, one thing you said, your general energy — and they fill in the rest with material from their own lives. The coworker who reads you as "arrogant" is often pattern-matching you to an arrogant brother. The one who reads you as "warm" may be doing the same thing with a favorite aunt.

This cuts both ways and that's the useful part. A harsh read of you is mostly them; a glowing read is mostly them too. You are supplying maybe 40% of any impression. Once you know this, individual opinions lose their power to define you, which is exactly the condition you need to look at them calmly.

## One reading is an anecdote. Three is data.

Never update your self-image off a single person's reaction. One person calling you "intense" tells you about that person. Three unrelated people — different contexts, no contact with each other — using the same word tells you something is actually landing that way. That's your pattern threshold. Below it, discard. At or above it, it's real and worth looking at.

This rule alone kills most perception-anxiety, because most of it is built on one bad interaction replayed forty times. Forty replays of one data point is still one data point.

## Never audit live

Monitoring yourself during a conversation is the same mechanism as a basketball player thinking about their elbow angle mid-free-throw. Attention that should run the behavior gets diverted to watching the behavior, the behavior degrades, and now you have real evidence of awkwardness — manufactured by the watching itself.

So: during the interaction, you are only in the interaction. Review afterward, once, briefly, like a coach watching game tape. If you catch yourself watching yourself mid-conversation, put your attention on the other person's face and their last sentence. Attention can only be one place; give it a job.

## Ask narrow questions

The global question — "what do people think of me?" — has no answer, so your brain fills the vacuum with its worst inventory. It is a bottomless pit by design.

Narrow questions have answers:

- Not "was I annoying at dinner?" but "did I talk over people at dinner?"
- Not "does my boss respect me?" but "in that meeting, did my point about the budget land or did it read as complaining?"

A narrow question returns a narrow answer, and narrow answers are survivable. You can hear "yeah, you interrupted Maria twice" and fix it by Tuesday. You cannot fix "annoying."

## Choose your sources like they're instruments

You can only metabolize feedback from someone whose motive you don't have to decode. If half your processing power is spent on *why are they telling me this*, the content never lands clean. So calibrate people with low stakes first: ask a small honest question ("was that email too blunt?") and watch whether they give you a real answer or a reflexive "no you're fine!" The ones who give real answers on small things are your instruments for big things. The reflexive reassurers are lovely people and useless sensors.

## The body flag protocol

Sometimes a piece of feedback hits and your body reacts before your mind does — heat in the face, stomach drop, an instant urge to explain yourself. That reaction means the feedback grazed old material, and in that state the information cannot be processed, only defended against.

The protocol: notice the body response, say "let me sit with that," and park it for 24–48 hours. Then look again. The feedback hasn't changed, but your nervous system has stood down, and now you can sort it — usually into "partly true, fixable" or "that was their projection." The flag isn't a malfunction. It's your best indicator of exactly where the blocks live, which means every flare is also a map.

## The whole thing in one line

Others' perception of you is low-resolution data about your behavior, filtered through their history — collect it in narrow doses, from calibrated sources, after the fact, and only trust patterns.

# What Do Other Kids Think of Me?
*A guide for when that question makes your tummy feel weird.*

---

## Why the question feels scary

A long, long time ago, if nobody in your group liked you, you couldn't survive on your own. So brains learned to treat "do they like me?" like a giant emergency alarm. Your brain still has that alarm. That's why wondering what people think can feel as scary as a growling dog, even though it's just a thought.

Here's the trick: the scared feeling is the *alarm* going off. It is not the *answer*. Fire alarms go off for burnt toast all the time. Feeling scared that everyone hates you doesn't mean everyone hates you. It means your alarm is loud.

## Nobody sees the whole you

When another kid thinks about you, they don't see all of you. They see a tiny little picture — like a sticker version of you — made from maybe three things: how you said hi, one thing you did at recess, and how loud you were. Then they fill in the rest with stuff from *their own life*.

If a kid thinks you're mean, a lot of times it's because you reminded them of their mean cousin. If a kid thinks you're funny, maybe you laugh like their favorite uncle. Their picture of you is half you and half *them*. So one kid's opinion is never the truth about you. It's a sticker.

## The Rule of Three

Here's a rule that fixes almost everything:

**One person saying something about you = a guess. Three different people saying the same thing = a clue.**

If one kid says you're bossy — that's just that one kid's sticker. Throw it away. But if your friend, your cousin, AND your teammate all say you're bossy, and they don't even know each other? That's a real clue. Then you can look at it and decide if you want to change something.

Most bad feelings about "what people think" come from remembering ONE bad moment over and over. Replaying it forty times doesn't make it forty clues. It's still one.

## Don't watch yourself while you play

Try walking down stairs while thinking really hard about exactly how your feet move. You'll trip! Feet work best when you don't watch them.

Talking works the same way. If you're at lunch thinking "am I being weird right now? Was that weird?" — your brain stops running the *talking* and starts running the *watching*, and THEN you get weird. The watching *causes* the thing you were scared of.

So: while you're with people, just be with people. Look at their face. Listen to what they said. If you want to think about how it went, do it later, for one minute, like watching a replay of a soccer goal. Then stop.

## Ask small questions, not giant ones

"Does everyone like me?" is a giant question with no answer. When your brain can't find an answer, it makes up a scary one. That's just what brains do with empty spaces.

Small questions have real answers:

- Not "was I annoying at the party?" — instead: "did I interrupt people at the party?"
- Not "does my teacher hate me?" — instead: "was I too loud during quiet time today?"

See the difference? You can't fix "annoying." But "I interrupted Maya twice"? You can fix that by tomorrow. Small questions give you jobs. Giant questions give you monsters.

## Find your honest person

Some people always say "no, you're fine!" no matter what. They're being nice, but they're like a broken thermometer — it always says the same number, so it can't tell you anything.

You need one honest person. Test them with something small: "was my joke actually funny or just loud?" If they give you a *real* answer, they're your honest person. Save your big questions for them.

## The hot-face rule

Sometimes someone says something about you and your face gets hot, or your stomach drops, or you want to yell "THAT'S NOT TRUE!" really fast.

That hot feeling means the words poked an old sore spot — like when someone bumps a bruise. And here's the thing: **you can't think straight while the bruise is throbbing.** So don't decide anything right then. Just say "hmm, okay" and walk away. Look at it again tomorrow.

Tomorrow, the words will be exactly the same, but the sting will be gone, and you can sort them into two piles: "a little bit true, I can work on it" or "that was their sticker, not me."

Bonus: every time your face gets hot, you just found a bruise you didn't know you had. That's actually useful. Now you know where it is.

## The whole thing in one sentence

Other people only see a sticker of you, so don't believe one opinion, don't watch yourself while you play, ask small questions to honest people, and if it stings — wait until tomorrow to look at it.

# What Do People Think of Me?
*A guide for a question that's harder than adults pretend it is.*

---

## The feeling and the answer are two different things

Start here, because everything else depends on it.

When you wonder "does she still like me after what I said?" — a bad feeling shows up fast. Stomach, chest, face. Most people treat that feeling as the answer: it feels bad, so it must BE bad, so she must be mad.

But the feeling arrived before any actual information did. It can't be the answer. It's your alarm system, and your alarm system has one job: guess the worst thing that could be true, in case you need to be ready for it. It got that job a very long time ago, when being left out of the group was actually dangerous, and back then guessing "they hate me" when they didn't only cost you some worry — but guessing "they like me" when they didn't could cost you everything. So the alarm learned to lie in one direction, on purpose, because that was the safer lie.

It still lies in that direction. Every time. Which means the feeling isn't wrong exactly — it's doing its old job well. It's just not the answer to your question. The answer has to come from somewhere else. The rest of this is about where.

## Nobody has seen the whole you — including the people who like you

Think about a kid in your class you don't know well. What do you actually know about them? Probably three things. The way they talk, one thing they did once, whether they seem loud or quiet. From those three things, your brain built a whole person — and it built the missing parts out of *other people you've known*. If they remind you of someone you like, you filled the gaps with good stuff. If they remind you of someone who was mean to you, you filled the gaps with that.

Everyone is doing this to you, right now, constantly. The kid who decided you're annoying built that partly out of you and partly out of some annoying person from their own life. You maybe supplied half of it. The rest was theirs.

Here's the part most guides skip because it's uncomfortable: **this is also true when people like you.** The friend who thinks you're hilarious is partly seeing you and partly seeing their funny older brother. You don't get to say "the bad opinions are their baggage but the good opinions are the pure truth about me." Same machine, both directions. Which is a little sad, and also freeing: no single opinion of you — good or bad — was ever the whole truth. They were all half-portraits.

Half, though. Not zero. You did supply something. That's why the next rule exists.

## One is a story. Three is a pattern.

If one kid says you're a bad sport when you lose — that could be about you, or about them, and you genuinely can't tell which from one data point. Set it down.

If your friend says it, and your sister says it, and a kid on your team says it — three different people, different places, who've never talked to each other — now the part that's *them* has been washed out. What's left is the part that's you. That's a pattern, and patterns are worth looking at.

Most of the pain around "what people think" comes from breaking this rule backwards: taking ONE bad moment and replaying it thirty times until it feels like thirty pieces of evidence. It's still one. Replaying doesn't multiply it.

But — and this is the nuance — a pattern being real doesn't automatically mean you should change. Say three people tell you you're "too loud," and all three of them are very quiet people. The pattern is real: quiet people find you loud. What you do with that is still your call. Maybe you dial it down around them. Maybe you decide loud is who you are and quiet people aren't your crowd. The pattern tells you what's happening. It doesn't tell you what to do about it. That part was always going to be yours.

## Watching yourself is what breaks you

Walk down stairs while thinking hard about exactly what your feet are doing. You'll stumble — not because your feet are bad, but because attention can only be in one place, and you moved it from *doing the walking* to *watching the walking*.

Now here's the trap, fully: you're at a sleepover, and you start wondering "am I being weird right now?" Your attention leaves the conversation and turns around to watch you. With nothing running the conversation, you actually DO get a little stiff and strange. And then your brain says "see?? I KNEW you were being weird!" — and it feels like proof. But you weren't weird until the watching started. **The checking manufactured the thing it was checking for.** This is the cruelest loop in the whole business, and knowing it's a loop is most of escaping it.

The escape isn't "stop thinking about it" — you can't delete a thought by ordering it to leave. The escape is that attention needs a *job*, so give it one: the other person's face, the exact thing they just said. Attention pointed at them can't be pointed at you. Same slot.

If you want to think about how you came across, fine — do it after, once, for a minute, like watching a replay. Then done. A replay watched once is learning. A replay watched thirty times is just the alarm feeding itself.

## Giant questions get monster answers

"Does everyone like me?" has no answer. Not a scary answer — *no* answer. "Everyone" isn't a person and "like" isn't one thing. But your brain hates an empty space where an answer should be, so it fills the hole with whatever's lying around, and what's lying around is the alarm system's inventory of worst cases. The monster wasn't found. It was *manufactured to fill a vacuum.* Ask a question with no answer, get a monster, every time.

Small questions have actual answers:

- Not "was I annoying at the party?" → "did I interrupt people at the party?"
- Not "does my teacher hate me?" → "did she seem annoyed when I asked to go to the bathroom the third time, or was she annoyed before I even asked?"

Notice what the small version does. "Annoying" is a verdict about your whole self — you can't fix a verdict. "I interrupted Maya twice" is a *behavior* — you can fix that by Thursday. The giant question asks *what am I*, and the small question asks *what did I do*, and only one of those has a handle you can grab.

## An honest person is an instrument. Most people aren't.

Ask most people "was that mean, what I said?" and they'll say "no! you're fine!" before you've finished the sentence. They're being kind. They're also useless — like a thermometer that reads 72° no matter what, you can't learn the temperature from it.

And there's a second problem, sneakier: if you ask someone whose motives you can't read — do they want something? are they still mad from yesterday? are they showing off? — then half your brain spends the whole time decoding *why they're saying it* instead of hearing *what they're saying*. The information never lands clean.

So you need one person whose answers you don't have to decode. Find them by testing small: ask something low-stakes — "was my joke actually funny or were people just being polite?" — and watch what comes back. A real answer, even a slightly ouchy one, means you've found an instrument. Guard that person. Save your real questions for them, and ignore the 72°-forever people on this topic (they're still fine for everything else).

## Hot face means wait, and hot face means dig here

Sometimes feedback lands and your body answers before your mind gets a turn — face goes hot, stomach drops, and the urge to shout "that's NOT TRUE" arrives at full speed.

Two things about that moment, and the second one matters more.

First: you cannot sort feedback while it's stinging. In that state your brain has exactly one setting — *defend* — and defended information never gets examined. So don't decide anything right then. "Hm, okay" and walk. Look again tomorrow. The words will be identical; the sting won't. Now you can sort it into the two honest piles: "partly true, and I can work on it" or "that was their half-portrait, not mine." Note that "partly true" is a legal answer. Feedback almost never sorts into fully-true or fully-false, and forcing it into one pile or the other is usually the alarm talking. Waiting isn't weakness or dodging. Sorting just doesn't work while the alarm is screaming, so you wait for quiet. That's timing, not fear.

Second, the interesting part: not everything makes your face go hot. Someone says your handwriting is messy — nothing. Someone says you were being a baby about losing — *flare.* The flare only fires where something already tender got pressed. Which means every flare is a flag on a map, marking a spot in you that was sore before this person ever opened their mouth. That's not a malfunction to be embarrassed about. It's the most accurate information in this whole guide, and it's about you, delivered by you. The people who eventually get sturdy about this stuff aren't the ones who stopped flaring. They're the ones who started reading the flares.

## All of it at once

Other people see half-portraits of you, painted partly from their own lives — so no single opinion is the truth, good ones included; only patterns count, and even patterns just inform your choice rather than make it. Don't watch yourself while you're living, ask small answerable questions instead of giant unanswerable ones, keep one honest person, and when it stings — wait a day, then sort. The sting itself is a map.

None of this makes the alarm stop going off. It goes off for everyone, forever, including every adult you know. The difference between people who are wrecked by it and people who aren't was never a quieter alarm. It's knowing the alarm isn't the answer.
