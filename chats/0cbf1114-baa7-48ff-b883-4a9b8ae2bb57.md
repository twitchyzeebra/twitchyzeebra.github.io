## Assistant

"Crazy" is one of those words that's been asked to do too many jobs, and the note — *sometimes, being crazy is helpful* — lives right at the fault line between them.

The space around this idea in the culture is thick. Apple's "here's to the crazy ones." Nixon's madman theory. The fool in *Lear*, who's the only one allowed to tell the king the truth. Cortés burning the ships. Every founder mythology ever written. The pattern underneath all of them is the same mechanism wearing different costumes: **crazy is what it looks like from the outside when someone stops pruning their options by social plausibility.**

That's the actual engine. Most people never evaluate most ideas — the ideas get killed *before* evaluation, by an internal filter asking "would a reasonable person do this?" Crazy is just running with that filter off. The cost is obvious: you now wade through genuinely bad ideas that the filter existed to kill. The benefit is the whole point: the filter is calibrated to consensus, and consensus is sometimes wrong, so the filter kills good ideas that merely *look* wrong. Crazy is the only way to reach those. Sane people can't get there — not because they lack the intelligence, but because the idea dies in them before it's ever conscious.

The "sometimes" in your note is doing real work, and here's the shape of it: crazy pays off in **convex** situations — where being wrong is cheap and being right is enormous. Pitch the insane idea in a brainstorm: worst case, mild embarrassment; best case, it's the one. Crazy is catastrophic in **concave** situations — where being wrong is ruinous and being right just gets you to normal. Don't be crazy about the landing gear. That's the whole rule, and most "embrace your madness" advice fails because it names the vibe and not the geometry.

Two other things live in this word's neighborhood worth flagging:

**Crazy as social license.** Being read as crazy costs you credibility but buys you freedom — low expectations, permission to deviate, the fool's exemption. Some people cultivate it deliberately: act unpredictable so opponents can't price you (that's the madman theory), or so nobody bothers to constrain you (that's every eccentric who's quietly getting away with it).

**Crazy as early.** "Sane" mostly means "calibrated to what everyone currently believes." When the consensus moves, yesterday's crazy gets retroactively renamed *visionary*. But Sagan's caveat sits right there: they laughed at Galileo, and they also laughed at Bozo the Clown. Most crazy ideas are just wrong. The word doesn't distinguish — only outcomes do, later, unfairly.

So the note compresses to something like: *the plausibility filter is a tool, not a law, and there are known conditions under which turning it off wins.* The interesting follow-up question for your map isn't whether crazy helps — it's whether you can tell, *from the inside*, which kind of situation you're standing in.
