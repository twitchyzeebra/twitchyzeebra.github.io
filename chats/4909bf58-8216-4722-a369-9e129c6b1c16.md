## Assistant

Taking "the space" as the whole ecosystem your clipboard guy sits in — money flow, incentives, and where it leaks.

**The chain (follow the $20):**

1. **You** — impulse, guilt, or research. The whole industry is built on which of those three moves you.
2. **The street fundraiser ("chugger")** — usually not charity staff. Employed by a fundraising agency, paid hourly plus quotas/commission on *monthly sign-ups*, not one-off donations. This is why he wants your bank details, not your fiver. A monthly donor is worth hundreds over their lifetime; cash in a bucket is worthless to his metrics.
3. **The fundraising agency** — a for-profit contractor. Charges the charity per acquired donor, often £80–150+ per sign-up. Your first year of donations frequently just repays your own acquisition cost.
4. **The charity** — splits money into program spend, admin, and *more fundraising*. The dirty ratio isn't admin (people fixate on it wrongly) — it's fundraising spend, which can be enormous and self-perpetuating.
5. **The implementing partner** — big-name charities often don't run projects themselves; they grant to local NGOs. Another layer, another cut, another place accountability blurs.
6. **The field** — where your instinct from last message hits: this is the part almost nobody upstream can actually show you, because they're four contracts removed from it.

**The incentive corruption at each node:**

- Fundraiser: paid for *sign-ups*, so guilt-scripts beat information. Truth slows conversion.
- Agency: paid for *volume*, so they optimize scripts, locations, targets (young people sign up more, cancel more).
- Charity: paid in *donations*, so they optimize emotion. Sanitized hope-porn or misery-porn, whichever tests better with their demographic. Reporting failures loses donors, so failures vanish.
- The whole chain: nobody in it is paid for *outcomes*. That's the structural rot.

**The models, on a spectrum of proof:**

- **Street/door/phone fundraising** — zero proof, maximum emotion, highest acquisition cost. What you met.
- **Brand charities (mass advertising)** — glossy annual reports, cherry-picked stories, real work often mixed in but unverifiable from outside.
- **Child sponsorship** — feels like proof ("your kid, Maria"), mostly fiction; money is pooled, the letters are a retention product.
- **Direct-give models (GiveDirectly)** — cash straight to recipients, published transfer data. Radical transparency, boring marketing.
- **Evaluated giving (GiveWell, effective altruism orbit)** — third parties rank charities on cost-per-outcome, publish spreadsheets and mistakes. Exactly your "show me what it's actually like" demand, institutionalized.
- **Local/direct involvement** — you fund something you can personally see. Highest verification, smallest scale.

**The donor failure modes (your side of the map):**

- Guilt-giving — pays for more guilt-marketing.
- Warm-glow giving — donating to feel good, indifferent to results; the industry's actual product.
- Overhead-obsession — refusing anything with admin costs, which punishes competent orgs and rewards ones that lie about their ratios.
- Cynical non-giving — "it's all corrupt so nothing" — comfortable, and false; the evaluated stuff demonstrably works.

**Where it nets out:** the street model exists because impulse converts and evidence doesn't. Everything you objected to — the guilt script, the missing proof, the sanitizing — isn't a bad apple, it's the equilibrium. The escape hatch is decoupling *the decision to give* from *the person asking*: decide amounts and targets on your own time with real data, then a guy with a tablet has nothing to leverage, because the question's already answered.
