# Ideality - Fable 5 Space v2

## User

## Assistant

**"Sometimes solutions to non-ideal problems, are non-ideal."**

## What it means to people

This sentence lives in two registers, and people reach for it from both.

**The permission-slip register.** Someone is judging their fix against a world that doesn't exist. The duct tape, the workaround, the harm-reduction program, the ugly compromise — all of these get attacked with "but ideally you'd..." The aphorism answers: *ideally* isn't the problem I was handed. This is the folk cousin of "don't let perfect be the enemy of good," "you go to war with the army you have," triage logic. People deploy it to defend a real solution against an imaginary standard.

**The warning register.** Read the other direction, it's a caution: don't expect the fix to feel clean, and don't be surprised when it carries costs. The methadone clinic, the ceasefire that rewards an aggressor, the tech debt you knowingly take on. The sentence pre-loads the disappointment so it doesn't read as failure.

## The mechanism — why it's actually true

Solutions inherit the constraint structure of their problems. A problem is non-ideal precisely *because* some constraint blocks the clean move — no time, no trust, no resources, a broken institution, a fait accompli. Any real solution has to live inside that same constraint, so the deformity passes through. You can't extract an ideal answer from a space that's already been bent.

Economics formalized this as the **theory of the second best** (Lipsey & Lancaster): in a system with multiple distortions, fixing one distortion while others remain can make outcomes *worse*. The genuinely best available move in a broken system can look wrong — even backwards — by ideal-world standards. Political philosophy did the same split with Rawls's **ideal vs. non-ideal theory**: the rules for a just society and the rules for getting there from injustice are different disciplines, and importing one's standards into the other is a category error.

## The space around it

The neighboring phrases in the training-data sense — "perfect is the enemy of good," "least bad option," "the map is not the territory," "worse is better" (the famous software essay) — all share one job: **licensing pragmatism without endorsing cynicism.** That's a narrow ledge, and the word doing the balancing in your version is *sometimes*.

Drop the "sometimes" and the sentence becomes an excuse machine — every corner cut, every ethical shortcut gets retroactively blessed as "non-ideal solution to a non-ideal problem." Realpolitik runs on the excuse-machine version. Keep the "sometimes" and it stays an *observation* rather than a license: the messiness of a solution is evidence-neutral. It might mean the problem was genuinely constrained. It might mean you stopped looking early. The aphorism refuses to tell you which — that's its honesty, and also why it needs a second tool next to it.

## The failure mode it doesn't mention

Non-ideal solutions calcify. The temporary fix becomes load-bearing; the workaround becomes the architecture; the emergency measure outlives the emergency. Nothing in the aphorism guards against this, because the aphorism is about *accepting* the non-ideal, and acceptance has no built-in expiry date. If your idea map grows a sibling card, that's the one it wants: *non-ideal solutions are answers to a problem-state, and problem-states move.*
