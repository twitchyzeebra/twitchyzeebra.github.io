# How You're Perceived: A Field Guide
*For people who want the information without the panic attack that usually comes bundled with it.*

---

## Why the question hurts in the first place

"How do people see me?" gets processed by the same brain machinery that once tracked whether the tribe was about to leave you behind. Social evaluation is filed under survival, so the brain doesn't answer the question neutrally — it answers with the worst plausible case, because for most of human history, underestimating rejection got you killed and overestimating it just made you cautious. Your anxiety is a smoke detector calibrated to go off for toast.

This means the raw feeling you get when you wonder how you came across is not information about how you came across. It's information about your threat system. Two different channels. Everything below is about reading the first channel without tripping the second.

## The thumbnail principle

People do not perceive you. They perceive a compressed thumbnail built from three or four data points — how you greeted them, one thing you said, your general energy — and they fill in the rest with material from their own lives. The coworker who reads you as "arrogant" is often pattern-matching you to an arrogant brother. The one who reads you as "warm" may be doing the same thing with a favorite aunt.

This cuts both ways and that's the useful part. A harsh read of you is mostly them; a glowing read is mostly them too. You are supplying maybe 40% of any impression. Once you know this, individual opinions lose their power to define you, which is exactly the condition you need to look at them calmly.

## One reading is an anecdote. Three is data.

Never update your self-image off a single person's reaction. One person calling you "intense" tells you about that person. Three unrelated people — different contexts, no contact with each other — using the same word tells you something is actually landing that way. That's your pattern threshold. Below it, discard. At or above it, it's real and worth looking at.

This rule alone kills most perception-anxiety, because most of it is built on one bad interaction replayed forty times. Forty replays of one data point is still one data point.

## Never audit live

Monitoring yourself during a conversation is the same mechanism as a basketball player thinking about their elbow angle mid-free-throw. Attention that should run the behavior gets diverted to watching the behavior, the behavior degrades, and now you have real evidence of awkwardness — manufactured by the watching itself.

So: during the interaction, you are only in the interaction. Review afterward, once, briefly, like a coach watching game tape. If you catch yourself watching yourself mid-conversation, put your attention on the other person's face and their last sentence. Attention can only be one place; give it a job.

## Ask narrow questions

The global question — "what do people think of me?" — has no answer, so your brain fills the vacuum with its worst inventory. It is a bottomless pit by design.

Narrow questions have answers:

- Not "was I annoying at dinner?" but "did I talk over people at dinner?"
- Not "does my boss respect me?" but "in that meeting, did my point about the budget land or did it read as complaining?"

A narrow question returns a narrow answer, and narrow answers are survivable. You can hear "yeah, you interrupted Maria twice" and fix it by Tuesday. You cannot fix "annoying."

## Choose your sources like they're instruments

You can only metabolize feedback from someone whose motive you don't have to decode. If half your processing power is spent on *why are they telling me this*, the content never lands clean. So calibrate people with low stakes first: ask a small honest question ("was that email too blunt?") and watch whether they give you a real answer or a reflexive "no you're fine!" The ones who give real answers on small things are your instruments for big things. The reflexive reassurers are lovely people and useless sensors.

## The body flag protocol

Sometimes a piece of feedback hits and your body reacts before your mind does — heat in the face, stomach drop, an instant urge to explain yourself. That reaction means the feedback grazed old material, and in that state the information cannot be processed, only defended against.

The protocol: notice the body response, say "let me sit with that," and park it for 24–48 hours. Then look again. The feedback hasn't changed, but your nervous system has stood down, and now you can sort it — usually into "partly true, fixable" or "that was their projection." The flag isn't a malfunction. It's your best indicator of exactly where the blocks live, which means every flare is also a map.

## The whole thing in one line

Others' perception of you is low-resolution data about your behavior, filtered through their history — collect it in narrow doses, from calibrated sources, after the fact, and only trust patterns.

## Attunement: reading their 60%

If you only supply 40% of an impression, the other 60% is their filter — their history, their mood, what the last person did to them before you walked in. Attunement is the skill of reading which filter is currently active and shaping your 40% to land in it. Same joke, two rooms: in one it reads as playful, in the other as a jab, and the joke didn't change. The filter did. An attuned person clocks the room before spending the joke.

## Why this doesn't violate "never audit live"

This is the part that matters, because on paper attunement sounds like exactly the self-monitoring the guide bans. It isn't, and the difference is the direction of the lens.

Auditing: attention pointed at yourself. *How am I coming across, was that weird, is my voice doing the thing.* This is the free-throw problem — attention that should run the behavior is watching the behavior instead.

Attunement: attention pointed at them. *She leaned back when I sped up. He lit up at the second topic, not the first. The room went quiet when I mentioned the deadline.* This is just observation of another person, which is what attention is built for. It doesn't degrade your performance because nothing is watching the performer. The performer is busy watching the audience — which, not coincidentally, is what every good performer actually does.

So the rule refines to: attention during an interaction goes outward, never inward. Auditing and attunement feel adjacent but they're opposite ends of the same lens.

## What you're actually reading

Not minds. Deltas. You can't know what someone's filter contains, but you can see when it engages:

- **Tempo shifts** — they were matching your pace and stopped. Something landed wrong or heavy.
- **Energy asymmetry** — you're at an 8 and they're at a 3. Whoever is higher usually needs to come down; dragging someone up rarely works and reads as pressure.
- **Topic ignition** — they gave two-word answers until one subject got a full paragraph. That's the door. Walk through it, not past it.
- **The withdrawal tell** — folded arms is overrated; the real one is answers getting shorter while agreement gets faster. "Yeah. Yeah, totally." means they've left.

None of this requires interpreting *why*. Why is inside their 60% and not your business mid-conversation. You just read the delta and adjust — slow down, come down, change subject, give room. Mechanic, not psychic.

## The trap

Attunement over-practiced by an anxious person collapses back into auditing, because the anxious brain uses "reading them" as a laundering scheme for "checking whether they hate me." The tell: if every delta you notice gets converted into a verdict about you, you're not attuned, you're auditing with extra steps. Real attunement produces adjustments. Fake attunement produces self-reports. Check which one you're generating and you know which mode you're in.
