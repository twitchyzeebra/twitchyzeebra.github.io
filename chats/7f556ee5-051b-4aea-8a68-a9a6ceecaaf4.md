Your note is just the title, so I'm analyzing the dichotomy itself.

**The framing is the first problem.** "Passion vs stability" presents them as opposing forces on one axis, but they're mostly independent variables. You can have a stable job you love, an unstable job you hate, and every combination between. The "vs" usually appears when someone is rationalizing a specific choice — staying in a safe job they resent, or justifying a risky leap — and needs the tradeoff to feel inevitable.

**What's actually being traded:**
- Not passion vs stability, but *variance vs predictability of outcomes*. Passion-driven paths have fat tails: some people get everything, most get little. Stable paths compress the distribution.
- The hidden variable is *runway*. Someone with savings, skills that transfer, or family backup isn't choosing between passion and stability — they're choosing between passion and *slightly more* stability. Someone with none is choosing between passion and eating. Same framing, completely different decision.

**The mechanisms people miss:**
1. **Passion is often downstream of competence, not upstream.** People report loving things they've gotten good at. So "follow your passion" reverses the causal arrow — build skill first, and passion frequently shows up.
2. **Stability decays if unattended.** The "safe" choice isn't static — industries die, roles get automated, the stable job in 2010 isn't stable now. Stability is maintained, not possessed.
3. **Passion monetized changes character.** Turning the thing you love into rent money attaches deadlines, clients, and metrics to it. Some people's passion survives this; many people's doesn't, and now they've lost both the income safety *and* the refuge.

**The practical resolution** is usually asymmetric bets: keep the stable base, run the passion at small scale until it produces evidence (income, audience, undeniable pull), then rebalance. The all-or-nothing version makes better stories, which is why it dominates the discourse and misleads people.
