Your note is one line, so I'll analyze the idea itself — what it gets right, where the mechanism actually lives, and where it breaks.

**What's true in it:** Chaos does one thing well — it forces recombination. Random input breaks your retrieval habits. Left alone, you pull from the same associations every time; noise injects material you wouldn't have selected, and unselected material is where the surprising connections come from. That's why cut-ups, oblique strategies, and constraint-scrambling work. It's not mystical, it's sampling outside your priors.

**Where the idea usually goes wrong:** People flatten "chaos as fuel" into "chaotic life = creative output." That's the romantic myth, and it's backwards. Chaos generates raw material; it doesn't finish anything. Every productive use of disorder pairs it with a stable editing process — someone sober enough, later, to sort signal from noise. The messy genius who ships work almost always has a hidden boring routine doing the actual conversion.

**The useful distinction:** chaos in the *input* (random prompts, unfamiliar fields, breaking your process on purpose) is cheap and high-yield. Chaos in the *conditions* (instability, crisis, no structure) mostly just taxes the executive function you need to make anything.

So the sharper version of your note: chaos is fuel, not an engine. It burns hot but only inside a structure that can contain the burn.
