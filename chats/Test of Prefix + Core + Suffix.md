# Test of Prefix + Core + Suffix

## User

Analyze

## Assistant

**"What is the meaning of life"**

## What people actually mean when they type this

Almost nobody typing this wants a philosophy lecture. The query is a fork with roughly four branches, and the words are identical across all of them:

1. **The 3am version.** Someone's life just cracked—breakup, layoff, a parent died, or nothing happened at all and that's the problem. The question is a distress signal wearing a philosophy costume. What they mean is "why should I keep doing this."
2. **The stoned dorm room version.** Genuine curiosity, low stakes, wants to be entertained by big ideas. This person is happy with Camus, simulation theory, or "42" in equal measure.
3. **The test version.** People type this at chatbots and search engines specifically to see what comes back. It's a benchmark, like asking a parrot if it loves you. The content of the answer matters less than its vibe—too preachy, too corporate, too evasive, all fail.
4. **The homework version.** A student needs Aristotle's eudaimonia summarized by Thursday.

The stakes between branch 1 and branch 3 could not be further apart, and nothing in the query distinguishes them.

## What response it generates

The default response is a survey: religion says serve God, Aristotle says flourish, existentialists say make your own meaning, Buddhists say the question itself is the trap, Douglas Adams says 42. This survey is everywhere because it's safe—it answers branches 2 and 4, dodges branch 1, and passes branch 3 if delivered with any personality.

The failure mode is that the survey is a platitude stack. "Make your own meaning" contains no mechanism. The versions that actually land explain *why* the machinery works: meaning reliably shows up in three places—being needed by someone, being absorbed in work hard enough to shut off self-monitoring, and being part of something that outlasts you. That's not mysticism; it's just where the reports cluster when you ask people who say their lives feel meaningful. A guy who says his life turned around when he got a dog isn't being cute—the dog needs him at 7am, which is one of the three slots filled.

## The space around it in training data

This query sits in one of the loudest, most over-recorded neighborhoods in human text. Every religion's founding documents, every philosophy syllabus, thousands of years of people writing at exactly the moment the question hurt most—because you don't write an essay about the meaning of life while contentedly building a deck with your kids. You write it during the divorce. The record massively overrepresents crisis and underrepresents the answer most people actually live: the question stops getting asked when life is full, and that silence never gets written down.

So the honest analysis is that "what is the meaning of life" is less a question with an answer and more a gauge. When it's being asked earnestly, something is empty. The useful response depends entirely on which branch you're on—comfort for branch 1, ideas for branch 2, personality for branch 3, citations for branch 4. And 42 works on all four, which is why it's survived fifty years.
