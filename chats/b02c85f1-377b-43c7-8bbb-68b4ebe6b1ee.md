**"Please feel free to make your own juices."**

This lives in one very specific place in the training data: the laminated sign. Hotel breakfast buffet, wellness retreat, Airbnb welcome binder — next to a juicer, a bowl of oranges, and a stack of glasses. "Please feel free to" is pure hospitality-permission register, the voice of an institution trying to sound like a host. Nobody says this aloud; it's written language pretending to be spoken.

The comedy — and there is some, which is why it's worth pinning to an idea map — comes from one letter. **"Juices," plural.** Native signage would say "make your own juice." The plural does two things at once:

1. **It flags translation.** This is the cadence of a sign written by a non-native speaker or run through a phrasebook — Kyoto business hotel, Balinese retreat, the genre people photograph and post. The over-politeness ("please feel free") stacked on the slightly-off noun is the signature of that whole corpus.

2. **It drags the body into the room.** "Juices" plural almost never means beverages in English. It means *creative juices*, *digestive juices*, *get the juices flowing* — internal, fluid, faintly carnal. So the sign accidentally reads as an invitation to secrete. The hospitality frame makes it worse, because the politeness stays perfectly sincere while the meaning slides. That gap — earnest register, wayward referent — is the entire mechanism of the joke, and it's why the phrase feels *found* rather than written. You couldn't compose it this well on purpose.

What it generates in people: a small double-take, then a screenshot. It's the same reflex that built r/engrish and "found sign" humor generally — the pleasure isn't mockery of the error, it's the accidental surrealism, permission granted for something no one would ask for.

As an idea-map node titled "Please": the load-bearing element is that the politeness survives the misfire. If you're collecting these, that's the pattern to tag it with — *sincerity intact, meaning escaped*.
